# ChronoFlow: An Electricity-Free, Volumetric, Clockwork-Governed Syringe Driver for Neonatal Infusion in Low-Resource Settings

**Category:** Biomedical Engineering (ENBM)

**Purpose.** Neonatal IV therapy in hospitals without reliable electricity relies on gravity drips or hand-pushed syringes, and the only non-electric alternatives meter flow by pressure through a restriction, so delivered rate changes with fluid viscosity, temperature and back-pressure. The goal was to design a non-electric syringe driver that meters *volume* to ±5 %, works in any orientation, offers selectable clinical rates (1–10 mL/h), runs a full syringe on one wind, and has mechanical occlusion and free-flow protection, for under $50.

**Procedure.** A parametric mechanical design was developed in CadQuery (91 parts, built in armed and tripped states, STEP/STL exported) and validated with physics-based models written for the project: torsion of thin pre-tensioned strips for the balance suspension, beam-column finite elements for an alternative flexure pivot, a fourth-order Runge–Kutta simulation of balance–escapement dynamics, Lewis tooth stress, a 20 000-sample Monte-Carlo error budget, a mechanism-level model of the escapement (re-cocking dynamics and lock impact over the torque range), and a 12-pose pairwise solid-interference check of the assembly.

**Results.** Energy analysis showed the escapement must operate over a 24× power range (17–416 µW across 5–60 mL syringes, 1–10 mL/h and 0–300 mmHg), which a conventional lever escapement cannot do without over-banking (simulated amplitude > 1.2 rad). A constant-force "spring-arm" escapement, adapted from the gravity escapement of tower clocks, holds amplitude constant at 0.59 rad and keeps period error below 0.06 % across the full range. A taut-band suspension made from a 0.0015" (38.1 µm) feeler-gauge steel ribbon, with a slotted clamp to absorb stock-thickness tolerance, gives a 1.20 Hz balance with ±0.19 % orientation sensitivity, no pivot friction and infinite fatigue life (166 MPa vs 350 MPa limit). A 44.5 N drive spring keeps escapement torque ≥ 2.0× the re-cock minimum in every syringe × rate × pressure cell, including a 60 mL syringe at its occlusion trip. Plug-in gear cassettes provide 1, 2, 5, 10 mL/h (0.4–19.5 mL/h across 5–60 mL syringes) with 0.12–1.16 µL per-beat resolution. Predicted accuracy: 95 % of units within ±1.14 % of set rate, versus +45 % (37 °C) and −33 % (100 mmHg back-pressure) for flow-restriction pumps. A fully modelled snap-action trip (0.35 mm sensing travel set by the sear geometry, spacer preload keyed to the syringe size) stops the oscillator at 300 mmHg (disclosed band 280–320 to 202–398 mmHg for plunger-friction uncertainty) and captures the pusher stem with a pin, verified on the solids, so no stored compression is released as a bolus; a latch interlock prevents free-flow. Bill of materials: $40.

**Conclusion.** Combining two historical precision-mechanics principles — the constant-force escapement and the taut-band suspension — with 3D printing yields a printable, electricity-free infusion driver whose predicted accuracy is limited by the syringe bore rather than by fluid, temperature, pressure or orientation. The next step is the gravimetric validation protocol (IEC 60601-2-24) included with the design.
